The old dock had not changed in the thirty years since I left it. The same pilings, green with rot and salt, leaned into the water at the same angle. The same smell — diesel, kelp, and something sweet and spoiled underneath — met me at the end of the road, and for a moment I was seventeen again, hauling traps beside my father while he whistled through the gap in his teeth.

But the boats were gone. Where a dozen trawlers had shouldered against each other, there was only the flat grey water and a single abandoned skiff, its hull stove in and its name sanded down to nothing. The cannery that had run three shifts a day, that had lit the whole harbour until midnight, stood hollow, its windows boarded with plywood the colour of wet cardboard.

My father would not have recognised it, and I found that I did not want him to. The town had sold its boats, then its cannery, then its young people, in that order, and had called each sale progress. Standing there, I understood that I had not come home. I had come to a place that had been carefully, deliberately emptied, and had been asked to call the emptiness a memory.
